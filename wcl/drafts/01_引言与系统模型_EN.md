## I. Introduction

Wireless sensor networks (WSNs) deployed over large or temporarily
inaccessible areas rely on unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) to collect sensing
data and relay it to a ground base station (RBS), forming a two-hop
GU–UAV–RBS architecture. In such systems the UAVs jointly decide their
trajectories, their transmission schedules toward the RBS, and the access and
transmission mode of the ground users (GUs), so as to maximize a system energy
efficiency objective, i.e., the amount of data actually delivered to the RBS
per unit of UAV energy consumed.

Hierarchical multi-agent deep reinforcement learning (MARL) is an effective
framework for this joint problem: an upper-level controller determines UAV
trajectory and scheduling, while a lower-level controller selects each GU's
access decision and radio mode (active RF or backscatter) [ref: Sensors 2023,
HMADDPG]. This decomposition matches the different time scales of the two
decision layers. However, the lower-layer DQN in such a hierarchy converges
slowly, requiring a large number of real environment interactions. The
original work explicitly identifies this bottleneck and suggests
*"integrating model-based local information to accelerate convergence"* of the
lower-layer controller [ref].

In this letter we respond to that call by augmenting the hierarchical MARL
framework with a lightweight Dyna-Q world model that performs virtual planning
for the lower-layer discrete decisions only. Our contributions are threefold:

1. **A local world model for the lower layer.** We train a compact feed-forward
   network (~8.5k parameters) as a surrogate of the GU buffer and energy
   dynamics, supervised by the closed-form physical equations of energy
   harvesting and data buffering, rather than by black-box latent prediction.
2. **Virtual planning restricted to the lower layer.** Dyna-Q planning is
   applied exclusively to the discrete access-control decisions, whose state
   transitions admit reliable closed-form models. The upper-layer continuous
   trajectory transitions are not used for virtual experience, avoiding error
   amplification in continuous spaces.
3. **An ablation separating the sources of gain.** A three-way comparison —
   single-layer versus hierarchical, with versus without model planning, and
   across planning depth K — isolates the contribution of the hierarchical
   architecture from that of the learned model.

The remainder of this letter is organized as follows. Section II presents the
system model, Section III describes the proposed method, Section IV reports
the experimental results, and Section V concludes the letter.

## II. System Model

### A. Network Model

We consider a UAV-assisted WSN consisting of $N$ UAVs, $M$ ground users (GUs),
and one ground base station (RBS) at the origin. Each GU generates sensing data
into a finite buffer. In each time slot, every UAV moves according to an
upper-layer action, serves a subset of GUs within its coverage radius, and may
schedule a downlink transmission of buffered data to the RBS. Each GU is served
by at most one UAV per slot. The system operates in a slotted manner with
slot durations $\tau_f$, $\tau_s$, and $\tau_d$ for UAV motion, GU
communication, and UAV–RBS transmission, respectively.

### B. Channel Model

The UAV–ground channel is modeled as Rician fading, capturing the strong
line-of-sight component typical of air-to-ground links. The channel vector
from UAV $i$ to a ground node is

$$ h = \sqrt{\psi(d)} \left( \sqrt{\frac{K}{1+K}} h_{LoS} + \sqrt{\frac{1}{1+K}} h_{NLoS} \right), \quad (1)$$

where $\psi(d) = \omega_0 d^{-\alpha}$ is the large-scale path loss at
distance $d$, $K$ is the Rician factor, $h_{LoS}$ is a deterministic unit-norm
LoS component, and $h_{NLoS}$ is a circularly symmetric complex Gaussian
component.

### C. Communication and Energy Model

Each GU operates in one of two modes. In **active RF mode**, the GU transmits
with power $p_m$ and its achievable rate toward its serving UAV is

$$ r_a = \tau_z \log_2\left(1 + \frac{p_m |h|^2}{\sigma^2}\right), \quad (2)$$

where $\tau_z = \tau_s / |\mathcal{C}_i|$ is the time share per served GU and
$\sigma^2$ is the noise power. In **backscatter mode**, the GU reflects the
incident UAV signal and transmits without consuming its own battery, with rate

$$ r_b = \tau_z \log_2\left(1 + \frac{p_A \Gamma_o^2 |h|^4}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad (3)$$

where $p_A$ is the UAV's excitation power and $\Gamma_o$ is the backscatter
efficiency. Simultaneously, a GU harvests energy from the incident
radio-frequency signal. Following the linear harvesting model of [ref], the
energy harvested by GU $m$ in a slot is

$$ E_h = \mu \, p_A \, \tau_z \, |h^{H} w|^2, \quad (4)$$

where $\mu$ is the energy conversion efficiency and $w$ the beamforming vector.
The GU energy evolves as $E_m \leftarrow \min(E_{max}, E_m + E_h - E_c)$,
where $E_c$ is the energy consumed by active transmission. [TBD: confirm
harvesting/consumption terms against the paper.]

The **UAV energy consumption** per slot consists of propulsion and
communication terms, $\epsilon_{UAV} = \epsilon_{prop} + \epsilon_{comm}$, with
propulsion energy $\epsilon_{prop} = P_0 (1 + 3 s^2 / v_{max}^2) \tau_f$ for
UAV speed $s$ and hovering power $P_0$ [Zeng 2019]. The UAV energy is used as
the denominator of the efficiency metric below; we do not model a battery
budget that constrains UAV operation.

### D. System Energy Efficiency

The objective of the system is the delivered-to-RBS data per unit of total UAV
energy, aligned with the system energy efficiency in [ref, Eq. (9)]:

$$ \Xi = \frac{\sum_t D_{rbs}(t)}{\sum_t \epsilon_{UAV}(t)}, \quad (5)$$

where $D_{rbs}(t)$ is the data actually delivered to the RBS in slot $t$.
